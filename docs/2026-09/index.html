Research

Papers with a publication date between Sept 1 and Sept 30. All are summarized from their PubMed abstracts; no full texts were read. Two of them (Cisneros, Breithaupt) got a one-line mention in No. 0 and get full cards here.

RCTn = 50One Texas municipal shelterFront. Vet. Sci. · Sept 3Open access · abstract read

Active enrichment, medication, and the combination all scored lower on arousal than baseline in shelter dogs

Cisneros et al. randomized dogs showing excessive arousal into five arms after a baseline week, then ran one week of treatment. Per the abstract, the baseline group had higher post-enrichment arousal scores than the active-enrichment, pharmaceutical, and pharmaceutical-plus-calm-enrichment groups; the calm-enrichment-only arm isn't listed as different. The combined arm had less lip licking, panting, vocalizing, and jumping than all other treatments.

“(4) pharmaceutical (20 mg/kg gabapentin, 6 mg/kg trazodone, 5-min walk 2x/day), and (5) pharmaceutical and calm enrichment (20 mg/kg gabapentin, 6 mg/kg trazodone, 15-min walk 2x/day, 15-min human-animal interaction)”

50 dogs over five arms, at a single shelter, for one week of treatment. The abstract doesn't give per-arm counts or say who scored arousal or whether they were blinded. In the arms above, walk length and human contact differ along with the drugs, so enrichment content and time are mixed together. Trazodone and gabapentin were also the drugs in the Delabar trial covered in No. 0, so don't treat the two as independent confirmation of each other. The authors declare no conflicts.

This is a pilot-sized signal that active enrichment, or gabapentin plus trazodone (alone or with calm enrichment), may lower arousal scores in dogs like these. It is not a protocol.

No stated designNo sampleAnimals · Sept 8Open access · abstract read

Shelters have temperament tests but no validated tool for how a dog is coping now

Breithaupt and Jacobs (Michigan State University) argue that shelter dogs are staying longer and that stress responses vary by dog. They say behavioral interventions may help and that some dogs could also benefit from medication.

“Most shelter behavior assessments focus on temperament testing to predict future behavior at home or identify individuals at risk for aggression, but few exist that can help identify generalized stress related to their current environment.”

The abstract describes no data collection or systematic search, so it's an argument, not a finding. The authors declare no conflicts of interest.

The authors say a validated assessment of current coping is needed. The paper gives you a framing, not a tool.

doi.org/10.3390/ani16182820 · PMID 42791675

Qualitativen = 54 + 18 forum threadsUK stakeholdersPLoS One · Sept 9Open access · abstract read

UK stakeholders say imported rescue dogs often didn't match adopter expectations

Wallis et al. interviewed or ran focus groups with overseas and UK rescue representatives, trainers, transporters, vets, officials, fosterers, and owners.

“Overall, caregiver expectations often did not align with reality after the dog arrived, leading to costly surprises for new owners.”

The abstract also says rescues were seen as having a moral responsibility to give accurate information and pre- and post-adoption support, but that this was “inconsistently provided.”

These are stakeholder perceptions, not measured rates of problems. It is a UK study of importation. The paper states “This research was funded by DEFRA including the post of LW,” and says the funder had “no role in study design, data collection and analysis, or decision to publish.” Among other disclosures, one author reports consulting for MARS Petcare, Royal Canin, and Forthglade.

For a San Diego shelter the takeaway is narrow: the study's participants saw weak expectation-setting and follow-up as a problem. It says nothing about local adopter outcomes.

doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0343416 · PMID 42715163

Prospective cohortn = 84 dogsClient-owned, mean age 12.77 yrsJ Vet Intern Med · Sept 1Open access · abstract read

In aging dogs, soiling tracked cognitive test performance, not just urinary findings

Rafatpanah Baigi et al. found soiling scores rose with age and fell with urine specific gravity. Higher soiling scores went with worse inhibitory-control and detour-task results. Abnormal urinalysis findings were less frequent than the authors expected.

“Soiling behavior in aging dogs might not exclusively reflect urinary tract or systemic disease and could be an indicator of cognitive decline.”

Owned dogs enrolled in an aging study, and the dementia rating scale (CADES) is owner-completed. These are associations only. No conflicts declared.

For a senior who is soiling in the kennel or foster, this is a reason to flag it to the vet as possibly cognitive as well as urinary. It's not a diagnosis.

doi.org/10.1093/jvimsj/aalag208 · PMID 42806282

Between-groups experimentn = 30 (15 + 15)Owned dogs, ArgentinaBehav Processes · Sept 14Abstract only

Owner presence dampened frustration behaviors during a food task

“Dogs were divided into two groups, one with the owner present (N = 15) and another with the owner absent (N = 15).”

Dogs with the owner present showed lower frustration/stress behaviors than the owner-absent group. The abstract says the owner's presence “did not affect any of the variables associated with problem solving.”

15 per group, owned pets in a task setting. The effect involved the dog's own owner, so it says nothing about unfamiliar staff or volunteers. No conflicts declared.

Hedge hard: this doesn't show that a shelter handler can do the same for a dog who doesn't know them.

doi.org/10.1016/j.beproc.2026.105439 · PMID 42735840

Pilot comparisonn = 21 dogs (12 pit bull-type)TaiwanAnimals · Sept 15Open access · abstract read

Two commercial DNA breed tests disagreed on pit bull-type labels

“substantial discordance was observed between the two services in the assignment of the American Pit Bull Terrier and American Staffordshire Terrier”

Samples went to Embark and Orivet. The authors conclude commercial DNA breed identification needs more validation before it can support regulatory enforcement.

n = 21 and a pilot. It is framed around enforcement in Taiwan, not US shelter practice, and it measures agreement between tests, not behavior. No conflicts declared; the abstract doesn't say who funded the test kits.

Two tests disagreeing on the same dogs in a 21-dog pilot is the whole finding. It doesn't say which test, if either, is right.

AB 1999 (Kalra): shelter veterinarian license and VCPR changes

No. 0 mentioned this only in a context box. Digital Democracy's summary says the bill creates a shelter veterinarian license, adds retired and retired-volunteer statuses, revises the veterinarian-client-patient relationship rules, and permits rabies vaccination without an established client relationship. The governor's Sept 14 list shows it signed.

Digital Democracy's text is a summary, not the statute, and I couldn't open the leginfo digest or text. Chapter number and status come from Digital Democracy only. No. 0 gave an effective date of Jan 1, 2027 from leginfo; I couldn't re-confirm it this run. I did not read any commentary on the bill, so who supported it isn't covered here.

If it works as summarized, it may change who can treat and vaccinate in a shelter. Check the actual text before changing any workflow.
