Research

Papers whose PubMed record or journal page shows a September 2026 date. Only one is a shelter-dog behavior trial; the shelter and rescue pool was thin this month. Every paper was summarized from its abstract (Cisneros also from the open-access full text).

RCTn = 50 dogs, 5 armsOne Texas municipal shelterFront. Vet. Sci. · Sept 3Open access · full text read

Medication plus calm enrichment cut arousal behaviors in shelter dogs

Cisneros et al. randomized dogs showing excessive arousal to five arms: baseline (a 5-minute walk twice a day), calm enrichment, active enrichment, medication (gabapentin 20 mg/kg plus trazodone 6 mg/kg), and medication plus calm enrichment. Data covered one week of baseline and one week of treatment. The combined group showed less lip licking, panting, vocalizing and jumping than every other arm. The medication-only group jumped less than baseline but showed more body shaking.

“During post-enrichment, the baseline group had higher arousal scores compared to active enrichment (p = 0.041), pharmaceutical (p = 0.001), and pharmaceutical and calm enrichment (p < 0.001) groups.”

Funded by an ASPCA research grant; the authors declare no competing interests. Power analysis gave about 10 dogs per arm, and 12 of 62 enrolled dogs were excluded before analysis. Dogs moved from pair to single housing for the study, which the authors say may have changed behavior regardless of treatment. Medication was given in peanut butter, spray cheese or wet food, and the authors say some improvement in the medicated arms “may partly be attributable” to that food. I found no placebo arm described. Treatment lasted one week, so long-term effects are unknown. The authors write that the care tested may exceed what many shelters can provide.

One shelter's trial suggests longer walks and in-kennel interaction plus medication may lower arousal more than a short walk alone. It doesn't show what the medication adds beyond the food it came in, or what happens after a week.

Review / argumentNo new dataAnimals · Sept 8Abstract only

Shelters lack a validated measure of how a dog is coping right now

Breithaupt and Jacobs (Michigan State University) argue that most shelter behavior assessments are temperament tests aimed at predicting behavior in a home or aggression risk, and that few measure generalized stress in the current environment. They say enrichment and training may reduce stress, that some dogs may benefit from medication, and that access to anxiolytics varies with staff training, experience and clinical staff.

“There remains a need for a validated behavior assessment that measures coping ability in a clinically meaningful way”

Only the abstract was readable (the MDPI page returned an access-denied error). It describes no systematic search and gives no study counts, and it argues for a new kind of tool. The record lists no conflicts of interest.

Use it as framing for what your assessments do and don't capture. It is not evidence that any intervention works.

UK stakeholders say imported rescue dogs often don't match owners' expectations

Wallis et al. ran 30 interviews and 4 focus groups with rescue representatives, trainers, transporters, vets, officials, fosterers and owners, plus forum threads, and identified seven themes. Some dogs were felt to be poorly matched to the household and to show challenging behaviors.

“The rescue organisation was perceived to have moral responsibilities to provide accurate information and pre-and post-adoption support, but this was felt to be inconsistently provided.”

These are perceptions, not measured outcomes, and the forum threads were purposively sampled. The funder was DEFRA, which also funded the lead author's post; the paper says funders had “no role in study design, data collection and analysis, or decision to publish.” The competing-interest statement lists several author ties: one has consulted for MARS Petcare, Royal Canin and Forthglade, one runs a Brucella canis awareness page, and one works at VetPartners. It is UK-specific.

The overlap with your work is expectation-setting and post-adoption support. These are UK views of importation, not San Diego data.

Prospective cohortn = 84 owned dogsMean age 12.77 yrsJ Vet Intern Med · Sept 29Abstract only

In aging dogs, soiling tracked with cognitive test performance, not just urine findings

Rafatpanah Baigi et al. collected 231 urinalyses. Soiling scores rose with age and with falling urine specific gravity, and higher soiling scores went with poorer performance on the inhibitory control and detour tasks. Abnormal urinalysis findings were less frequent than expected.

“Soiling behavior in aging dogs might not exclusively reflect urinary tract or systemic disease and could be an indicator of cognitive decline.”

These are client-owned research-cohort dogs, not shelter dogs. The soiling and dementia-scale scores were owner-completed, and the findings are associations. The record lists no conflicts of interest.

With a senior intake who soils, this supports considering both a medical workup and cognition. It doesn't replace the workup.

California law

Chapter numbers and dates below come from the governor's lists and CalMatters Digital Democracy; leginfo blocked automated access, so none of it was checked there.

AB 2344 (Haney): faster path out of the kennel for seized animals

The bill extends the 14-day abandonment process to animals seized under any provision of California law. Digital Democracy's summary describes it as applying to “animals seized pursuant to any provision of California law”.

The two news pieces I could read quote the bill's author, the LA County DA's office and Social Compassion in Legislation, an advocacy group; I found no independent analysis. Contra Costa News (Oct 3, after the window) lists San Diego Humane Society among the supporters; the Spokesman-Review does not mention any opposition. Dates conflict: the Spokesman-Review (Sept 26) says Newsom signed it Friday, which was Sept 25, while FastDemocracy and Digital Democracy show chaptering on Sept 27 and the governor's list is dated Sept 27. I couldn't resolve the signing date without leginfo, so I list Sept 27 as the chaptering date. The 79–0 figure is the Assembly concurrence vote on Aug 31 as shown by FastDemocracy; the Assembly vote was 78–0 and the Senate 40–0.

Evidence and cruelty-case dogs may spend less time held, starting in January. How fast depends on local process.

Signed Sept 14 (governor's list)Ch. 205Effective Jan 1, 2027

AB 1999 (Kalra): a shelter-only veterinary license and new VCPR rules

dvm360 reports the new shelter veterinarian license covers veterinarians who are “employed by or contract with an animal shelter”, and that holders “may practice only for a shelter or under specified emergency circumstances”. It lists the Veterinary Medical Board, CVMA, San Diego Humane Society and Social Compassion in Legislation as co-sponsors and says the bill passed without opposition. Digital Democracy's summary adds time limits on the vet-client-patient relationship: one year for in-person exams or premises visits and six months for synchronous audio-video.

The co-sponsor list comes from dvm360, not from an independent source, and SDHS was one of the sponsors. Dates conflict: the governor's list is dated Sept 14, while Digital Democracy shows it chaptered Sept 15. Those can both be true. I used the governor's list for the signing date. Addition to No. 1: No. 1 listed this bill as signed Sept 14 without a chapter number; it is Ch. 205 per Digital Democracy and dvm360.

Shelters that struggle to hire vets may get a wider pool starting in January. How the license will work in practice isn't in what I read.

AB 2010 (Soria): high-volume spay/neuter no longer needs a separate surgical room

“[h]igh-volume spay or neuter services, as defined, shall not be required to be performed in a separate surgical room”

The wording is from Digital Democracy's summary, not leginfo. I found no effective date on any page I read. No. 1 said “Expected Jan 1, 2027”, which is an assumption. I found no independent estimate of how much clinic capacity this adds.

More spay/neuter capacity could mean fewer litters coming in over time. That is a hope, not something the sources show.

Signed Sept 30Ch. 1025Operative Apr 1, 2027

SB 1296 (Durazo): landlords must publish their pet policy up front

Digital Democracy says the bill requires landlords to “maintain written pet policies or pet addendums” and disclose them online, in digital ads and through rental search engines. It provides for a refund of application fees “upon request” if the policy wasn't disclosed, and it preserves existing service and support animal protections.

It requires disclosure only; nothing I read says it gives tenants a right to keep a dog. I didn't open a second source on this bill this run.

Adopters can check a rental's pet rules before applying. Whether that reduces returns is untested.

HoodlineSept 18

The county's new Santee shelter is designed to replace Bonita

Hoodline describes a $37 million, roughly 23,000 sq ft facility that replaces Bonita. It credits only the line that Bonita “operated for roughly 60 years and has faced escalating maintenance costs” to a Patch report, and it says the shelter “is designated to function as a regional emergency deployment hub and intake site during disasters.” Hoodline gave no opening date.

This is secondary reporting; the $37 million figure appears only in Hoodline, and the County page I read doesn't state a cost or capacity.

Correction to No. 1: No. 1 wrote “Bonita is closed.” The County's Oct 1 release says “County Animal Services will open its new Santee Animal Shelter on Oct. 15” and that “The opening will mark the permanent closure of the County's Bonita shelter.” I couldn't confirm that the closure has actually happened. The release also says the new shelter serves unincorporated county areas and that Santee city continues contracting with SDHS for animal control. The release is dated after this window.

Per the County, Bonita closes with the Oct 15 Santee opening. Check with County staff before telling adopters or transfer partners where to go.
